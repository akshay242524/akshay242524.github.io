ABOUT ME

Somewhere along the way, I got hooked on a question: what if the firmware could learn from its own behavior? Our modems produced a huge amount of trace data, and most of it only got a second look when something broke. I started training PyTorch and XGBoost models on those traces to predict better scheduling and power decisions. The real challenge turned out to be the last mile: getting a model small and fast enough to run on constrained ARM hardware without getting in the firmware's way. When it finally clicked, on-device responsiveness improved by about half.

None of that happened alone. The best problems came out of conversations with hardware, firmware, and use-case teams, people who knew exactly where performance hurt but didn't have a clean way to measure or predict it. Part of my job was turning those pain points into something a model could learn from, and then tracking down the right data to train it on. I also looked after the lab side, building Python and MATLAB tools for firmware flashing, RF measurements, and trace collection, so engineers could spend their time debugging instead of babysitting test runs.

Working across that many teams on shared release dates taught me something I still hold myself to: a feature isn't done when it compiles. It's done when it lands cleanly next to everyone else's work.

After MSD, I went back to school and earned my Master's in Computer Science at UNC Charlotte with a 4.0 GPA. I'm now a Research Associate there, building an offline Android app whose C++ and OpenCV pipeline has to give the same answer on very different phones. It's a different domain, but it's the same kind of problem I've always liked: getting real software to behave reliably on real, imperfect hardware.
